RELATED WORK AND CONTRIBUTIONS

Centralized prioritized planning

Guo et al.'s ICARCV 2024 method provides the closest baseline for this work.
It frames large-scale AGV scheduling as a prioritized planning problem: tasks
are assigned and vehicle routes are planned in priority order, with conflicts
resolved relative to higher-priority plans. This sequential structure offers
a practical way to construct schedules without jointly searching every
vehicle's route, but makes outcomes dependent on the priority order and
requires a mechanism for handling conflicts along routes. The baseline
considered here follows the same overall approach and notation.
https://doi.org/10.1109/ICARCV63323.2024.10821683

Centralized conflict-based search offers a different tradeoff. Andreychuk et
al.'s continuous-time CBS methods handle nonuniform action durations and
geometric collisions, with optimality guarantees for their respective
objectives; their experiments include instances with dozens of agents under
a 30-second limit. He et al. extend CBS to elevators, modeling single-agent
elevator capacity and cross-floor conflicts. Their 2026 preprint reports
improved success over standard CBS on multi-floor instances, while also
identifying growing runtime overhead as floor count increases. These methods
provide strong conflict reasoning, but do not decompose AGV planning into
floor-level servers.
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0004370222000029
https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20512

Decentralized and distributed multi-robot coordination

Distributed manufacturing schedulers address reliance on a single controller
by assigning decision-making to multiple resource-specific agents. Zhou et al.
propose collaborative reinforcement-learning schedulers for heterogeneous
smart-factory units, using real-time information and evaluating responses to
rush orders and machine failures. Their focus is online production scheduling
across resources rather than multi-floor AGV traffic coordination, and the
reported description does not establish a fleet-size limit. Maoudj et al.
likewise formulate a capacitated multi-AGV scheduling problem and propose a
decentralized multi-agent solution, demonstrating competitive results in
their manufacturing setting. Neither work, as described in the available
paper summaries, centers on assigning lift reservations across
floor-specific traffic planners.
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0736584521000855
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S073658452200196X

Hierarchical AGV scheduling with floor or zone decomposition

Hierarchical coordination decomposes traffic management by spatial scale or
zone. Zhao et al.'s spare-zone method assumes fixed vehicle paths and
allocates temporary zones for local collision and deadlock resolution.
Pratissoli et al. organize industrial traffic management into topological,
path-planning, and roadmap layers, using time-expanded graphs for coordination.
Their evaluation includes an industrial scenario with 2-8 AGVs; the authors
motivate scalability, while noting the computational difficulty of coupled
planning as fleets grow. Verma et al.'s dynamic resource reservation method
permits multiple reservations at shared resource points and can be implemented
centrally or semi-decentrally. These approaches establish the value of layered
traffic control and local conflict-resolution areas, but do not describe the
specific combination of floor-server route planning and centrally reserved
lift trips used here.
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1569190X21000228
https://doi.org/10.1109/TASE.2023.3276233
https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2024.3362293

Learning-based and metaheuristic AGV scheduling

Learning and metaheuristic methods target scheduling quality and
computational practicality. Wang et al.'s multi-state scheduling algorithm
predicts travel time with a neural network and reports application in an
air-conditioner production line. Hu et al. combine reservation timetables
with variable-neighborhood search for multi-load AGVs, reporting experiments
with 3-200 vehicles in dense networks. In general MAPF, Li et al.'s MAPF-LNS2
repairs collisions by repeatedly replanning subsets of agents; the authors
report results up to 8,000 agents on a congested warehouse map with a one-hour
limit. These results demonstrate that learning and search can improve
scheduling or scale to large fleets, but the cited approaches do not provide
the same floor-server decomposition with explicit central lift booking and
lobby waits.
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278612522001091
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1366554522000217
https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/21266

This work extends Guo et al.'s prioritized-planning baseline into a hybrid
central-plus-floor-server architecture. The central component assigns jobs,
divides routes into floor legs, and books lift trips; each floor server plans
traffic within its floor. Lift-lobby waiting points let vehicles wait near
floor-level conflicts, while central lift reservations coordinate cross-floor
movements. The design therefore combines prioritized planning with spatial
decomposition and explicit lift coordination. Its reported comparisons are
specific to the implemented factory layout and scenarios, rather than a
general scalability guarantee.
